# Style dimensions: what to look for when reading a sample

Use this as a lens during the close-reading pass. You will not fill every section - a
poet, a technical columnist and a novelist are distinguished by different things. Spend
your effort where the sample is *most distinctive*, and skip dimensions where it is
generic. Every observation should be backed by something you can point to in the text.

## Contents
1. Voice and stance
2. Sentence architecture
3. Diction and register
4. Rhetoric and imagery
5. Structure and movement
6. Content moves (how ideas get handled)
7. Fiction-specific
8. Language-specific notes (Arabic, German, English, Romance languages, others)
9. Separating style from content

---

## 1. Voice and stance
- Who is speaking? Narrator, essayist, teacher, friend, prosecutor, witness? How close to the reader?
- Point of view: first person singular / plural, second person address, impersonal "one".
- Distance and attitude: intimate, ironic, reverent, detached, combative, playful, weary.
- How much does the writer reveal about themselves? Do they admit doubt, or always sound sure?
- How does the reader get treated: as equal, as student, as accomplice, as adversary?

## 2. Sentence architecture
- Length profile: not the average alone but the *mix*. Long-long-long-short? Steady medium? Fragments used for punch?
- Syntax: paratactic (clauses stacked with "and") or hypotactic (subordination, nested clauses)? Front-loaded or periodic sentences (the point arrives last)?
- Openers: what do sentences start with - subject, adverb, conjunction, a question, a condition?
- Rhythm devices: triads, parallelism, repetition, anaphora, deliberate run-ons, one-line paragraphs.
- Punctuation personality: dash lover, semicolon user, colon-then-reveal, parenthetical asides, ellipses, rhetorical questions.
- Verb habits: active vs passive, tense choices (present tense for narration?), modality (must / might / could).

## 3. Diction and register
- Register: formal, conversational, technical, literary, colloquial, dialect. Does it shift mid-text, and when?
- Vocabulary texture: plain and Anglo-Saxon/Germanic vs Latinate/abstract; concrete nouns vs abstractions.
- Signature words or phrases that recur (verify by frequency AND that they are not just topic words).
- Words the writer *avoids* (hedges, jargon, clichés, adverbs, intensifiers like "very", "really").
- Borrowings and code-switching: foreign terms, slang, quotations in another language.
- Naming habits: how are people, places, concepts introduced and re-referenced?

## 4. Rhetoric and imagery
- Metaphor source domains: where do images come from (the body, machines, weather, war, cooking, religion)?
- Similes vs metaphors; extended conceits vs quick flashes.
- Irony, understatement, hyperbole, paradox, aphorism, list-making, analogy.
- Humor: dry, absurd, self-deprecating, sarcastic; where it is placed (punchline at the end of a paragraph?).
- Allusion and intertext: Quran, Bible, poetry, pop culture, classical references.
- Sound: alliteration, assonance, saj' / rhyming prose, cadence when read aloud.

## 5. Structure and movement
- Openings: anecdote, provocation, definition, scene, question, confession, statistic?
- How a paragraph is built: claim first and then support, or build-up and reveal? Typical paragraph size.
- Transitions: explicit connectors ("however", "لكن", "aber") or juxtaposition and white space?
- Endings: circle back, quiet image, call to action, open question, punchline?
- Macro-shape of a whole piece: sections, headings, numbered lists, epigraphs, digressions.
- Use of formatting: headings, bullets, bold, italics - or none at all.

## 6. Content moves (how ideas get handled)
- How is an argument made: example first, principle first, dialogue with an imagined objector?
- Evidence types: personal anecdote, data, authority, history, thought experiment.
- Handling of complexity: simplify, complicate, reframe, invert the common view.
- Recurring themes and preoccupations (these are content, but *how* they surface is style).
- What is left unsaid: does it explain everything or trust the reader?

## 7. Fiction-specific
- Narration: person, tense, reliability, interiority vs external action, free indirect discourse.
- Dialogue: tags ("said" vs varied verbs), dialect rendering, how much of the text is speech, subtext.
- Description: what gets described, at what density, through which senses.
- Pacing: scene vs summary, time jumps, chapter endings, how tension is released.
- Character voice separation: do characters speak differently from the narrator?

## 8. Language-specific notes

**Arabic**
- Register spectrum: فصحى تراثية / فصحى معاصرة / فصحى مبسطة / عامية (شامية، مصرية، خليجية...) / مزيج. Note where the register shifts (e.g. narration in فصحى, dialogue in عامية).
- Sentence style: long chained clauses with و/ف/ثم versus short modern sentences; use of الحال, المفعول المطلق, الجمل الاعتراضية.
- Rhetoric: سجع, طباق, جناس, تكرار, تشبيه, استعارة, كناية; direct address (يا ابني), rhetorical questions.
- Orthography habits: full or partial tashkeel, punctuation conventions (، ؛ ؟), quotation marks « » vs " ".
- Dialect writing: note spelling choices for spoken words (e.g. how "هلق", "شو", "كتير" are written) - consistency matters more than any particular convention.

**German**
- Sentence frame (Satzklammer), nominal style vs verbal style, compound-noun density, Konjunktiv II for hedging or reported speech.
- Register markers: du / Sie, Umgangssprache, particles (halt, eben, ja, doch), Anglicisms.
- Punctuation: comma rules make clause boundaries visible - long sentences can still be readable; note deliberate breaks.

**English**
- Latinate vs plain vocabulary, contractions, sentence fragments, phrasal verbs, spelling variant (US/UK).

**French / Spanish / Portuguese / Italian**
- Address: tu/vous, tú/usted/vos, tu/você, tu/Lei - and whether it shifts. Regional variants (e.g. Rioplatense voseo, European vs Brazilian Portuguese) are style decisions.
- Subject pronouns are often dropped in Spanish, Italian and Portuguese, so person lives in verb endings - read for it, the metrics will undercount it.
- Punctuation conventions: French spaces before `; : ? !` and « guillemets », Spanish ¿ ¡ openers, dialogue with em-dashes (—) rather than quote marks.
- Tense for narration (passé simple vs passé composé, pretérito vs presente histórico) and sentence length (Romance prose tolerates long subordinate chains).

**Other languages**
- The metrics still measure rhythm, paragraphs and punctuation, but person counts are skipped and phrase lists may include function words. Lean more on close reading. For Chinese and Japanese, lengths are counted in characters.

## 9. Separating style from content

Before recording a trait, run the **swap test**: *if the topic changed completely, would this trait still be there?*

- "Writes about exile and memory" -> content. Do not record as style.
- "Opens with a small concrete scene and reaches the abstract idea only in paragraph three" -> style.
- Frequent words like the protagonist's name or the subject's jargon -> content.
- Frequent *function-level* habits ("Here is what I believe", "und doch", "ولكن...") -> style.

Also check **consistency**: a trait that appears once is an accident, a trait that appears
in most sections is a habit. Note frequency ("almost every paragraph", "about once per page",
"only at emotional peaks") because dosage is part of the style.
